On the Role of Solid Lubricant
Fillers in the Tribological,
Micromechanical, and
Morphological Properties of PA66
Composites
To achieve thermoplastics-based composites with high tribological performances, molybde-
num disulﬁde (MoS2) and graphite (Gr) were incorporated separately to a polyamide 66
(PA66) matrix. Two different development methodologies of composites were used. The
morphological features and the micro-mechanical properties of composites were investi-
gated to stress the inﬂuence of the ﬁller surface treatment and its dispersion. The friction
coefﬁcient was evaluated using a ball on a ﬂat micro-tribometer. The wear mechanisms
were also characterized. The best tribological performance was exhibited by 10 wt% of
graphite. The surface treatment of the ﬁller inclusions by 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane
(AMPTES) showed an improvement in the morphological aspects of adhesion at the
ﬁller/matrix interface. [DOI: 10.1115/1.4045804]
Keywords: thermoplastics-based composites, MoS2, graphite, tribological performance,
micro-mechanical properties, adhesion, dry friction, wear, wear mechanisms
1
Introduction
The study of the wear behavior of polymer-based systems has
attracted the attention of many researchers [1,2] owing to their
wider availability of material choice, ease of manufacturing, light-
weight, and good strength. The highest efﬁciency of such material’s
adoption is found in situations where sliding wear is involved [3–5].
For not long ago, the polymer-based materials were favored over
their metal-based counterparts because of their low friction coefﬁ-
cient and capacity to sustain loads. This reason has given an
impetus to the industrial manufacture of the materials as in the pro-
duction of bearing components adopted in automotive industries
such as cams, vacuum pumps, wheels, etc.
In this article, PA66 is selected as a target material. Polyamide 66
(PA66) composites are ideal materials for dynamic bearing applica-
tions, even at high temperatures. They are widely used in automotive
applications such as automatic gears, sealing for turbocompressors
pumps, pedal and gear shift components, and valve covers. The
excellent mechanical properties, combined with the good wear resis-
tance, extend their range of uses to the aerospace industry as it offers
weight savings in compressor blades for turbojet engines [6].
In severe service conditions (i.e., very low or high temperature,
radiation, vacuum, extreme contact pressure), solid lubricants are
the best choice to control the friction and wear of thermoplastics.
When present at a sliding contact, solid lubricants act in the same
way as their liquid counterparts. Particularly, they shear easily to
prevent wear damage in the sliding interface and to reduce the fric-
tion coefﬁcient [7–9]. Several inorganic materials (e.g., graphite,
molybdenum disulﬁde “MoS2,” boric acid, hexagonal boron
nitride) can provide excellent anti-friction and wear ability. Their
lamellar or layered structure confers lubricity to most of these
solids. Recent advances indicated that the black phosphorus (BP)
was selected as a new two-dimensional material with intriguing
mechanical and lubrication properties. The incorporation of BP
nanosheets into organic (polyetheretherketone (PEEK)/polytetra-
ﬂuoroethylene (PTFE)) composites and inorganic (carbon ﬁber/
PTFE) composites could reduce friction and wear of both systems
[10]. For BP/polyvinylidene diﬂuoride (PVDF) composites, it was
reported that BP content larger than 10 wt% enhanced the friction
properties and the wear resistance of the composites [11]. Puértolas
et al. [12] studied the lubricant ability of graphene nanoplatelets
(GNPs) in improving the PEEK wear properties. The GNPs were
used without any chemical functionalization in thermoplastic-based
materials, the results showed an enhancement in the surface hard-
ness and in the tribological properties. Zhang et al. [13] reported
that aramid particles played a synergetic effect with sulfur dioxide
(SiO2) and PTFE nanoparticles in improving the tribological perfor-
mance of polyoxymethylene (POM) composites.
Graphite and MoS2 are natural minerals with lamellar structure
extracted from deposits around the world. The lubricity of graphite
lasts up to 400 °C, while MoS2 decomposes at about 200 °C.
Recently, partially graphitized carbon composites are developed

hand, MoS2 has the best overall load-carrying capacity as ﬁlms
deposited on rigid substrates and it is considered among the most

Nevertheless, the deployment of particle-reinforced polymer
matrix composites as components for use in real service needs a
good understanding of the processing associated with the structure
and its consequence on physical and mechanical properties. In this
ﬁeld, most recent research studies have provided a theoretical guide
and experimental tools to speciﬁcally tailor the matrix/ﬁller mor-
phology and surface chemistry in order to improve their adhesion
and decline the propensity for agglomeration [15,16]. Wu et al.
[17] have chemically treated the graphite powders to make them
well compatibility with the polyamide 6 matrix and they studied
the mechanical and electrical properties of the resultant composites.
The bending and tensile strengths were improved by the incorpora-
tion of a modiﬁed graphite powder. Although the tribological prop-
erties of thermoplastic composites ﬁlled with different reinforcing
ﬁllers have been extensively studied, the effect of the development
methodology and the ﬁller’s surface treatment on micro-mechanical
and morphological properties was not systematically compared yet.
The purpose of this study is to investigate the effect of adding
untreated and treated MoS2 and graphite with 3-aminopropyl-
triethoxysilane (AMPTES) to a PA66 matrix. In this context, two
development methodologies are proposed. The tribological beha-
vior of PA-based composites is reported. Micro-mechanical charac-
terizations and morphological aspects of the elaborated composites
are also studied in order to stress the effect of the processing meth-
odology on composites properties.

Experimental Details
2.1
Materials. The composites investigated in the present
study consisted of two ﬂaky shaped solid lubricant powders incor-
porated separately PA66 granules as the matrix material. The
polymer was provided by Mapex (1.14 g/cm3 density, 250 °C
melting point) and dried for 3 h at 85 °C.
The used ﬁller materials were MoS2 powder (6 μm average size,
5.06 g/cm3 density) with 95% purity and graphite powder of
average particle size in the range of 22 µm (1.9 g/cm3 density).
The

materials
were
obtained
from

(Sigma-Aldrich; Figs. 1(a) and 1(b)).
Different compositions were considered in this work in the range
of 2, 4, 7, and 10 wt% graphite or MoS2.
2.2
Composites Preparation. The composites preparation
was achieved with two methodologies. Concerning the ﬁrst conven-
tional methodology (M1), specimens were produced by direct inject-
ing molding using Demag Ergotech 125/475 industrial injection-
molding machine. The Shore A hardness of neat PA specimens
was94.66 ± 0.57 and Ra(the average roughness) wasabout 0.42 µm.
As for the second methodology (M2), solid lubricant powders
were chemically treated. The surface treatment of MoS2 particles
was carried out in a suspension containing 5 g MoS2, 10 ml
ethanol, and 0.05 ml 3-aminopropyltriethoxy silane (AMPTES)
(Sigma-Aldrich, 0.949 g/ml density). The suspension was stirred
mechanically (RZR1, Heidolph) for 45 min, then dried by
vacuum evaporation for 3 h. With respect to the surface treatment
of graphite, it was achieved by stirring 5 g graphite with 25 ml
ethanol and 0.015 ml AMPTES. The stirred solution was vacuum-
dried for 6 h. The blending of the polymer with varying weight con-
tents of treated solid lubricants was achieved by mechanical mixing
using an internal mixer with two blade impellers. Thereafter, spec-
imens were produced using an Arburg Allrounder 220 S injection-
molding machine, the surface roughness of neat PA_M2 was about
Ra = 0.36 and the Shore A hardness was 92.00 ± 1.63. The reason
for using the two methodologies is to compare the industrial man-
ufacturing process of composites by using direct injection
molding via Demag Ergotech industrial injection machine with
the second development methodology at the laboratory scale
including surface treatment of the ﬁller and mechanical mixing
with the polymer. The Arburg Allrounder injection-molding
machine provides processing with small shot weights.
2.3
Friction and Wear Testing. A reciprocating ball on a ﬂat
micro-tribometer was used for friction and wear tests. The descrip-
tion of the homemade device was published in a previous research
work [7]. A mechanical arrangement generated the required contact
load. The magnitude of the dead load can be adjusted to very low
values. The tangential load was monitored by a measuring head
which was equilibrated by a counterbalancing arm. The system
was based upon a translation table including precise linear ball
bearing and enabling the micro-displacement of the ﬂat specimens.
In order to avoid any mechanical vibrations of the system, all the
mechanical elements were ﬁxed on an inertial marble structure
with damping devices. A computer based software ensured the
treatment of the measurements and the display of the results.
A high-chromium steel ball with a diameter of 19.0 mm and an
initial surface roughness of Ra = 0.12 µm served as a counterpart.
The ball slides reciprocally against the ﬂat composites at a fre-
quency of 1 Hz over a displacement magnitude of 2 mm. The tribo-
logical investigations were performed with a constant normal load
of FN = 5 N up to 10,000 sliding cycles (peak Hertzian contact pres-
sure in the ball on ﬂat contact of about 35 MPa). For a better com-
parison between the different composites, at least three friction tests
were conducted for each set of conditions.
The wear scar shape was measured with an Inﬁnite-Focus Alicona
system at the end of each test. Before wear volume measurement, the
samples were ultrasonically cleaned to eliminate the wear debris.
In order to analyze wear mechanisms, scanning electron micro-
scope (SEM) observations (JEOL JSM 6400F) were also conducted
on the wear track coated with gold.
2.4
Micro-Mechanical
Characterization:
Scratch
Test.
Polymer scratch performance has drawn signiﬁcant interest in
recent years. Particularly, the scratch behavior of thermoplastic
composites was investigated in previous works [18–23]. In this
context, it was reported that the structure-property relationship
between morphology and scratch behavior in the case of polypro-
pylene showed ironing and surface plowing mechanisms in the
deformation mode [23–25]. It is hypothesized that the progression
of scratch damage mechanisms is related to the ﬁsh scale and
plowing transitions. The ﬁrst transition is marked by the appearance
of ﬂuctuations and a gradual increase in the tangential force mea-
sured by the scratch machine. In the second transition, abrupt
changes and large waves were seen in the load proﬁle [21,26].
The micro-mechanical analyses of composites were carried out
by means of the Revetest Automatic Scratch Tester (Institut Jean
Lamour-Département N2EV) which performs scratches under
either constant or linearly increasing load.
Tests were drawn over samples using a sphero-conical diamond
indenter of 120 deg cone angle and 138 µm radius spherical tip,
under continually increasing load. The loading initial value was
1 N, the rate was 14.5 N/min, and the scratching speed was
2.5 mm/min. The indenter is moved over the sample surface with
a linearly increasing load until a normal load of 30 N is reached
(Fig. 2). Tangential friction force, friction coefﬁcient, penetration,
and residual depth were continually monitored. The abrupt increase
in the load is termed the “critical load” for the onset of scratch vis-
ibility; this critical value was used to study the material plowing
resistance [21,24,27,28].

identify the fundamental failure modes. As new composites have
been developed, this technique is considered as a reliable tool for

2.5.2
X-Ray Tomography. X-ray tomography makes use of the
refraction of X-rays by the specimen in image formation. Image
processing relies entirely on X-ray absorption by the analyzed spe-
cimen as a mechanism of contrast formation. These types of phase-
contrast imaging methods warrant the visualization of details in
weakly absorbing materials such as polymers or objects that do
not absorb X-rays at all. In the context of materials science, X-ray
phase-contrast imaging has become an important technique for
the 2D/3D construction of low-density materials. It allows material
characterization in terms of voids and cracks detection. It also deter-
mines particle size and spatial distribution of the ﬁller. Hence, the
X-ray computed tomography is considered as a reliable tool to char-
acterize the performance of our composites.
The principal experimental parameters of the Phoenix-X-ray
device used (vtome x-S) are an X-ray tube voltage of 80 kV, a
scan time of approximately 1 h, a silicon-based detector is amor-
phous with 1920 × 1536 pixels of size, and the resolution adopted
is 3 µm.
3
Results and Discussion
3.1
Friction and Wear Behaviors of the Composites
3.1.1
Friction Testing Results. Figure 3 shows the friction
coefﬁcient of typical PA66 composites ﬁlled with solid lubricant
powders by the two development methodologies as a function of
sliding cycles (total sliding cycles of 10,000). These composites
contain different concentrations of solid lubricant powders. The
frictional behavior is to some extent reﬂected in this friction data.
The friction coefﬁcients of composites with 10 wt% Gr prepared
with M1 and with M2 achieve a relatively stable steady-state after
a short running-in phase [7]. However, the friction coefﬁcient
values did not stabilize as a function of the sliding cycles and
showed a continuously increasing trend for most of the tested spec-
imens. For pure PA, an increase in friction coefﬁcient is seen with
the second methodology. It can also be seen that the friction coef-
ﬁcient decreases as a function of the graphite content with both
methodologies.
In Fig. 4, friction coefﬁcients measured after 10,000 sliding
cycles for all PA composites are given. Meanwhile, the composites
manufactured by direct injection molding give rise to the lowest
friction coefﬁcient. Interestingly, the composite with 10 wt% of
graphite exhibits the lowest coefﬁcient of friction which falls
from 0.19 with pure PA to 0.06. At the same graphite content, the
friction coefﬁcient is 0.16 for the composites manufactured by the
second methodology. In the case of PA/MoS2 composites injected
and molded directly, the frictional coefﬁcient decreased slightly
as a function of the solid lubricant content up to 7 wt% after
which it increased. This is in agreement with the results published
by Wang et al., who ﬁlled metakaolinite-based geopolymer with
PTFE particles [30]. An optimum ﬁller fraction contributes to the
shearing force’s reduction between the surfaces in contact,
leading to a better friction response. As compared with direct injec-
tion molding, the frictional coefﬁcient of PA/MoS2 composites can
further be reduced by the second methodology at 10 wt% of the
ﬁller. In the present case, PA composite ﬁlled with graphite ﬂakes
and injected molded directly demonstrated the best improvement
in the friction performance.
3.1.2
Wear Test Results. Figure 5 shows the changes in the
wear volume loss with respect to the composite’s preparation
method, nature, and the proportion of the incorporated ﬁller into
the PA matrix. An overall decreasing trend of the wear volume
loss can be observed with the increase in wt% of the solid lubricant
ﬁller. The decreasing tendencies obviously differ for each different
condition. The best result is exhibited by the composition with
10 wt% of graphite, with a wear volume loss of 11.79 × 10−3
mm3, which is about six times lower than the value measured for
the pure PA. It is well known that graphite can be easily dragged
out from the matrix to form a third body transfer ﬁlm dispersed
on the sliding counterparts [31,32]. The quantity of graphite on
the sliding surface increases directly with its amount added to the
composites. The plenteous graphite ﬂakes spread symmetrically
on the sliding surface, which decreases the direct contact between
the composites and the steel counterpart [33]. It is also worth point-
ing out that wear volume loss of composites was higher with the
second manufacturing methodology that may be attributed to the
processing conditions (including mechanical mixing under electric

3.2
Scratch Test Results. Figure 8 illustrates the typical
results of the scratch test performed on pure PA and plots applied
force (left side) and vertical displacement (right side) versus
scratch distance. This ﬁgure reveals that the tangential force curve
does not present a neck during the scratch test. Hence, there is no
point in the curves that may identify a clear onset of failure,
which can be explained by the absence of any ductile plow
scratch damage with increasing load [40]. It can also be suggested
that the polymer was progressively damaged during the scratch test
[21].
Figure 9 shows the typical critical load of four composites (PA/
Gr_M1, PA/Gr_M2, PA/MoS2_M1, and PA/MoS2_M2) at 4 wt%
and 10 wt%, respectively, whereby each histogram has an
average of 10 different measurements. It was reported that the
improved scratch resistance of thermoplastic polymers leads to
higher critical loads [24]. As can be seen from the ﬁgure, the PA/
Gr composites elaborated with the ﬁrst methodology exhibit the
minimum values of the critical load, the average values are signiﬁ-
cantly improved with the second methodology. At 10 wt% of
graphite, the critical load rises from 7.4 N to 18.6 N. The load
values decrease as the solid lubricant percentage increases. It was
proved that undesirable failure mechanisms present during scratch-
ing are evident points strongly related to the role of the ﬁller parti-
cles in the scratch behavior [21,25]. It was also suggested that the
critical load is related to fracture criteria and some yielding as a spe-
ciﬁc failure mode [23]. Debonding of the solid lubricant particles
and the formation of voids would probably lead to the decreasing
tendencies in the scratch load as a function of the ﬁller content
[21,23]. Wong et al. showed that talc-ﬁlled PP was found to be
responsible for the great increased scratch visibility of PP due to
the massive formation of voids as well as crack and debonding phe-
nomena on the scratched surface [21]. The best results of the critical
load are given with PA/MoS2 elaborated with the second manufac-
turing methodology.

3.3
Fractography Analysis. Matrix fracture is used in order to
qualitatively assess the microscopical features of the composites
after a brittle fracture. In Fig. 10, the SEM micrographs recorded

4
Conclusion
The present work was undertaken with an objective to explore the
potential of the solid lubricant inclusions in PA-based composites
and to study the friction and wear properties of the developed com-
posites. This research work has focused on providing basic knowl-
edge to encourage further research in this area. The analysis of the
processing methodology and its dependence on the tribological
behavior, scratch response, and ﬁller dispersion leads to the follow-
ing conclusions and recommendations:
(1) Severe adhesive wear was observed on pure PA. The
polymer exhibited a high friction coefﬁcient and wear
volume loss.
(2) For composites injected molded directly, the incorporation of
the two solid lubricant powders, particularly graphite, was
beneﬁcial in reducing the friction coefﬁcient and the wear
volume loss of PA.
(3) Composite ﬁlled with 10 wt% graphite corresponded to the
best tribological parameters. The adhesive and plowing
wear was extremely reduced.
(4) The second development methodology, including surface
modiﬁcation of the particles by AMPTES, mechanical
mixing with the PA matrix, and microinjection, did not
improve the friction and wear results.
(5) The means performed to characterize the ﬁller/matrix adhe-
sion for the composites developed conventionally (M1)
showed poor interfacial adhesion with debonding and frac-
ture phenomena. Tomographic visualization in 3D showed
ﬁller agglomeration as well as the abundance of a large
number of gas bubbles trapped in the composites.
(6) The scratch test parameters were improved with the second
development methodology particularly with the MoS2
ﬁller. SEM micrographs of the fracture exhibited an
improved interfacial adhesion between the ﬁller and the
matrix. Debonding and voids were clearly reduced. X-ray
tomography features showed homogeneous dispersion of
the solid lubricant particles in the thermoplastic composites,
a signiﬁcant decrease in the density of the gas bubbles was
also noticed.
